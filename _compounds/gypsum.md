---
title: Gypsum
formula: CaSO4(H2O)2
found: >-
  Evaporite beds left where seas and salt lakes dried up, usually with halite and anhydrite, its
  waterless twin: across the Permian of Texas, New Mexico and Oklahoma, in Nova Scotia, and under
  Paris, whose Montmartre quarries gave plaster of Paris its name. White Sands in New Mexico is 700 km²
  of gypsum dunes. The clear crystal is selenite, the fine-grained rock is alabaster, and the Cave of
  Crystals at Naica in Mexico holds selenite beams eleven meters long.
mined: >-
  Quarried in open pits; it is so soft (2 on the Mohs scale, scratched by a fingernail) that it is
  ripped loose by bulldozer rather than blasted. Crushed and heated to about 150 °C it gives up three
  quarters of its water and becomes plaster of Paris, which sets back into gypsum when mixed with water:
  that is drywall, plaster and the retarder ground into every bag of cement. Power stations that scrub
  sulfur dioxide from their flue gas with limestone make gypsum too, and much drywall is made from it.
---
The Naica crystals grew for hundreds of thousands of years in water held at 58 °C, just below the
temperature where anhydrite dissolves and gypsum forms, and were only found in 2000 when the mine
pumped the chamber dry. The structure is sheets of calcium and sulfate held together by layers of
water molecules, bonded only through their hydrogens; the sheets peel apart along the water, which is
why selenite splits into thin clear plates.
