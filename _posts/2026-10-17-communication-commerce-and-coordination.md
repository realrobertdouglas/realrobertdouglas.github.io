Communication lets independent actors learn whether they can work together. When people can send messages, keep records, and check what actually happened, they can judge who delivers and who does not. That judgment is what makes commerce practical. Once commerce is practical, violence becomes less attractive, because attacking a trading partner also ends the benefits that partner was providing.

Reputation forms wherever parties can communicate more than once and remember the results. A trader who fulfills agreements is sought out again. One who breaks them is avoided. The information does not have to be perfect or immediate. It only has to be good enough that the value of future deals outweighs the short-term gain from taking something by force. Violence remains the final authority—the capacity to impose an outcome when nothing else works—but it stops being the ordinary method once reliable exchange is possible. Communication is what makes the alternative visible.

Distance changes how communication works without removing the need for it. Across light-years, a message may take years to arrive. Continuous command is impossible. What can still travel is technical knowledge, standard agreements, records of performance, and scientific results. A group that has learned how to draw energy from a star or convert ordinary matter into needed materials can transmit the methods. Others who have local energy and fabrication capacity can apply them without ongoing direction from the source. Communication here acts as a shared store of working knowledge rather than a channel for orders. Projecting force across the same distances stays expensive; moving usable information is cheaper and improves with better storage and error correction.

The same pattern appears on smaller scales. Early groups form where natural energy sources and diverse living systems—plants, animals, soils, and supporting minerals—are already concentrated. Communication inside and between those groups allows specialization. One place improves tools for local materials; another improves ways to store surplus; both gain by exchanging. When the easy local options run out, further growth requires either new energy or the ability to turn available matter into whatever is required. Knowledge of how to do that travels by the same routes that once carried simpler techniques. Groups that can receive and use the knowledge depend less on any single natural deposit. Groups that cannot remain tied to whatever scarce resources are left—the same resources that have historically been easiest for rulers to control.

Communication and commerce therefore undercut the scarcity rulers rely on. While converting matter is costly and the methods are tightly held, control of the original resource still gives leverage. Once the techniques spread and local energy is sufficient to use them, a particular deposit loses its unique value. What stays scarce is organized capacity: working equipment and the habits that keep it from being destroyed or seized by force. Those habits come from repeated communication—shared ways to measure results, methods for settling disputes short of violence, and records that distinguish reliable partners from unreliable ones.

Communication does not have to be free or everyone honest. It only has to make the returns from continued exchange clearer than the returns from raiding for enough people. When that holds, systems without permanent rulers can last. They last not because conflict disappears, but because the information needed to prefer trade is available and the cost of imposing rule from far away is not. Whether on a planet or between stars, the practical limit on authority is both the difficulty of projecting force and the relative ease with which communication can support coordination instead of command.
