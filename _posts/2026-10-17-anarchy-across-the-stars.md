Greek *anarchos* (“without authority” or “without rulers”). Anarchism as a political philosophy holds that government is both unnecessary and harmful, and that people can live justly and cooperatively without it.

Suppose humanity one day meets others. A galactic council forms. Delegates arrive, treaties are drafted, and a seat is offered. For a time the arrangement might hold—shared scientific data, mutual non-aggression pacts, coordinated responses to threats that actually span multiple systems. Yet the geometry of spacetime undercuts the premise of durable central rule. Information cannot travel faster than light. A message to a colony twenty light-years distant takes twenty years to arrive and twenty more for the reply. Orders issued from a capital are already decades out of date when they reach the edge. Fleets sent to enforce compliance consume resources on a scale that almost never matches the taxes, resources, or loyalty that can be extracted from the target. At sufficient distance the cost of governing exceeds the value of governing. What remains is local order or local disorder.

This is not merely a technical inconvenience. It is a structural limit. Any polity large enough to encompass systems separated by years of light-time will either fragment into self-governing units or expend itself trying to prevent that fragmentation. The second option is expensive and brittle; the first is the default once the travel time exceeds the half-life of political consensus. Anarchism, in this setting, is less an ideology than an engineering observation: beyond a certain radius, rulers cannot reliably project authority, so arrangements that do not require them become the ones that survive.

The practical question is therefore how to arrange those local systems so that the absence of rulers does not default to predation. Violence remains the final authority. Every other form of authority—custom, contract, currency, reputation—either rests on a credible capacity for force or on the shared expectation that force will not be used. Where that expectation collapses, the remaining options are submission or fight. The task is to keep the expectation intact for as long as possible.

Reliable trade is one of the more effective ways to do so. When two parties know they can exchange goods, data, or specialized labor on repeated occasions, each has a standing reason not to destroy the other. A raid may yield a one-time haul; it also ends the stream of future exchanges and advertises to every other potential partner that the raider is a bad counterparty. In environments where populations are sparse, specialized skills are scarce, and capital is costly to move, that lost stream is often more valuable than the plunder. The incentive calculation tilts toward continued commerce rather than conquest. Over time, the parties that internalize this—through reputation systems, escrow arrangements, mutual insurance, or simply the habit of keeping accounts—become the ones others prefer to deal with. Those that do not become isolated, which is itself a form of cost.

None of this eliminates the capacity for violence. It changes the conditions under which violence is the rational choice. Clear, transferable claims to resources reduce the occasions on which parties must fight to establish who owns what. Predictable methods for resolving disputes—arbitration that both sides have reason to accept, or simple exit when they do not—lower the chance that disagreements escalate to force. Open entry into trade and production makes predation less attractive relative to production. These are not guarantees; they are arrangements that raise the relative price of violence. Where they are absent, distance alone does not produce peace. It produces isolated strongmen or mutual raiding until someone invents the missing pieces.

The same logic applies whether the “aliens” are literal or merely the inhabitants of the next valley, the next continent, or the next orbital habitat. Scale changes the numbers; it does not change the underlying constraint. Authority that cannot be cheaply enforced will not be stably exercised. The remaining choice is between order that emerges from repeated voluntary exchange and order imposed by whoever happens to hold the local monopoly on force at any moment. The former is more pleasant for everyone who is not that monopolist, and it is more stable precisely because it does not require continuous projection of power across distances that make such projection ruinous.

Anarchism, read this way, is not the claim that conflict disappears. It is the claim that the institutions capable of containing conflict without permanent rulers are both possible and, beyond a certain scale, necessary. The galactic council may still exist for the questions that actually require coordination across light-years. Everything else will be settled closer to home, by people who have more to gain from trading with their neighbors than from ruling them.
