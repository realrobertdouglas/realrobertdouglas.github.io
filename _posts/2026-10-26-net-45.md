*Names have been changed. The companies, the town and the county are left out, and the dates are rounded. The bond amounts, the days counted and the outcome come from the county's public record. The rest is the plumber's account.*

In January of 2026 a builder finally picked up the phone.

The plumber on the other end had been calling builders for years. Most did not call back. This one did, and when the plumber asked about work, the builder said he had heard about his issues with the children. He would not say who had told him.

Children. There had been one accuser. One arrest. No indictment, and a case the county had marked declined a year and a half before that call. Four years on, it was still plural.

This is the story of how that happens to a plumber. It starts, as most things in the trade do, with an account at a supply house.

## The account

A plumber does not own much of what he installs. The pipe, the fittings and the water heaters come off a supply house counter on credit, and he pays the supply house when the builder pays him. Call him Ray. Ray had it in writing that he got forty-five days.

Late in 2015, or it may have been 2016, Ray and a partner ran a small shop. A few builders, some custom homes, and a rule about not growing too fast. So when the purchasing manager for one of the national builders called him in and offered him a neighborhood, Ray said he would take one. Only one.

A week later the offer had changed. Three neighborhoods to start, then every house the builder had in town. The two outfits doing that work were behind with their suppliers, the man said. They were robbing Peter to pay Paul, and the builder was getting notices.

"Your checks will be a quarter million dollars a month," he said.

Ray told him the truth. It could put him out of business as easily as it could make him rich. He said he would run the numbers.

He had been gone less than two hours when the man who ran the supply house called and asked him to stop by.

The supply man did not want to talk about pricing. He wanted to talk about the two plumbers Ray would be replacing. One of them owed the supply house something like three hundred thousand dollars. The other was a month behind. If Ray took their work, the supply house would never see that money.

"That's not my problem," Ray said. "I didn't go after this work. They came to me."

"Your account's due. You're at thirty days."

"I've got forty-five."

"You take that work on, I'm going to shut your account down."

Ray walked out to his truck. He had not left the parking lot when his partner called to ask why their account had just been closed.

Within days, one of the plumbers he would have replaced was telling people Ray did his jobs with stolen material and had no supply accounts left. Ray's shop moved its buying across town and paid off what it owed, somewhere between fifty and eighty thousand dollars. Then Ray called the builder and said the offer had already cost him too much. Somebody suggested another meeting. Somebody said no. Nothing was ever put in front of a judge.

The forty-five days had been in writing. It became thirty the moment someone with more leverage needed it to.

## The phone

About six years later Ray had his own company. In February of 2022 it collected ninety thousand dollars. That month the police arrested him.

A fourteen-year-old girl who had been living with his family had made an accusation, and the charge was the worst kind there is. He said it was not true. Bond was set at half a million dollars.

He sat in the county jail for seventy-seven days. The police kept his phone. Every customer he had was in that phone and nowhere else, so even the ones who might have waited for him never got a call. The month after his arrest, with him in a cell, the company still took in a hundred and forty-six thousand dollars. Then the bids went away, and then the jobs he had already been awarded, and then the ones still pending.

At the end of April a judge cut the bond to twenty-five thousand dollars, by agreement, and he walked out the next day. The bondsman had him check in once a week.

## The counter

His ride dropped him at his twin brother's office the afternoon he got out. Dale had a secretary then, and she had something to tell him.

While Ray was locked up, she said, she had been at a bar with the man from the supply house and a few others. He had told the table that the two of them, Ray and Dale, had gone to jail for raping a bunch of little girls, ten or twelve years old.

There had been one accuser and one arrest. Around the same time, Dale's wife's ex-husband had accused Dale of molesting his daughter. It was looked into and nothing came of it. Dale was never arrested for anything.

Ray's lawyer had warned him that people would say all kinds of things and that he should not get mad. Near the end of that year two people he knew came to him anyway. They had been standing in the supply house, they said, and heard the man say it out loud in front of everybody.

Them Pruitt twins aren't around anymore. They're all in jail.

Ray had been out for eight months.

He drove down there. The offices were on the second floor, and there were cameras. Nobody came down. He let it go.

## The paper

The charge stayed open for two and a half years. In that time Ray tried to read the accusation against him, and could not.

He asked the police for the affidavit that had put him in jail. They refused. He filed an open records request. It was denied and sent to the state. Three months later he called and was told the police were protecting the victim. He told them there was no victim. He asked what charge they had written down. An assault charge, they said, and would not say more. He never got a booking sheet. He never got the phone back.

No grand jury ever indicted him. In the late summer of 2024 the county marked the case declined, and the email he got said only that it had been declined for prosecution.

That is all the paper will ever say. It does not say cleared. It does not say innocent. It says declined.

## The call

Ray kept calling builders, and builders did not call back, and then in January of 2026 one did, and said the thing about the children.

By then Ray was operating manager and master plumber for another company. When that company opened its supply accounts, he had asked one question: whether the man from the supply house still worked there. He was told no.

Then his phone rang. It was the man. He was president of the supply house now, and he said he was their account rep and wanted to help them out.

Ray told him everything he had heard over the years. The man denied all of it. He said he would tell him to his face, the way he would tell anybody. He did not have to hide behind anything.

Ray told him to lose the number.

## The clocks

In the fall of 2026 the whole story was laid out against the Texas statutes, to see what could still be done with it.

A man who has been lied about has one year to take it to court, counted from the day the lie was told. Interfering with his business gets two years. A broken credit agreement gets four. Every one of those clocks had run out before anyone thought to ask the two witnesses to sign their names to what they heard. The paper that said forty-five days was gone too, lost when the old company was rolled up into another one.

So here is the arithmetic of the trade. A supply house gives a plumber forty-five days to pay for his copper. The law gives him one year to find out who has been talking about him. The man who sells him pipe on credit has other customers who owe him money, and a counter where a name gets passed around with its owner nowhere near it. And the only clock that ran while Ray knew about it was the one on his own account.

Ray is still a master plumber.

## What to take from it

1. **The man who holds your account holds your name.** He has other customers who owe him money and a counter where things get said. Keep accounts at more than one house. Keep your customer list in more than one place, because a phone can be taken and not given back. Keep your own copy of every agreement, somewhere that does not go away when the company does.

2. **"Not my problem" is usually true and never enough.** Other men's debts closed Ray's account. A charge that was never tried closed his company. Being right about whose problem it is does not keep it from landing on you. Plan for the day it lands, not for the argument about whose fault it is.

3. **You cannot answer what you cannot see.** The words that cost Ray the most were said where he was not, and the law's clock starts the day they are said, not the day he hears them. When someone tells you what was said about you, write down who, where and when, that day, and ask them to sign it. Do it while it still feels like overreacting. By the time it matters, the year is gone.

4. **What is said about you is not the record. What you did is.** Ray asked for one neighborhood. He paid the account when it was shut. He asked to see what he was accused of. He drove to the man's face. Nobody at the counter will keep that record for you. Keep it yourself, and keep it plain.

5. **When it is your turn to talk about someone, do the opposite of the man at the counter.** Say what you know. Name where you got it. Leave the rest as what other people said. That is the whole difference between him and this article, and it is the one lesson here that costs nothing.
