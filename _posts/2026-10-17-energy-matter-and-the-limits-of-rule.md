Energy is the capacity to rearrange the world. Some arrangements of matter already contain it in usable concentrations: coal beds, oil reservoirs, uranium deposits, falling water, sunlight captured by plants. These are not evenly distributed. A species that starts with access to such concentrations, and to the materials needed to extract and use them, develops faster than one that does not. The early surplus is biological as much as technical.

Diverse flora and fauna, together with the soils, waters, and mineral substrates that support them, supply calories, materials, and the work living systems do for free, all at relatively low energy cost. A landscape that already contains both the energy source and the living systems able to process it into food, fiber, and tools gives a population runway. It can grow, specialize, and accumulate knowledge before it must invent high-cost substitutes for what nature already provides.

That runway is finite. Local diversity can be depleted, soils exhausted, accessible deposits mined out, or the climate shifted beyond the range the existing ecology tolerates. When the low-cost options are gone, further development requires either new energy sources or the ability to convert whatever matter is on hand into the matter that is needed. Sufficient energy makes the second path available. Fission, fusion, or any process that releases a large fraction of a fuel’s rest mass can, in principle, power the transformations that turn common elements into rarer ones and bulk rock into structured materials. Once the energy budget is large enough and the conversion processes are reliable, the distinction between “resource” and “waste” collapses. Asteroids, comets, and planetary crust become feedstock. A star system is no longer a collection of specific deposits; it is a reservoir of atoms that can be reordered.

Primitive development therefore clusters where natural concentrations already exist and where the energy required to use them is modest. Advanced development detaches from those concentrations. A civilization that can move from star to star, or even from asteroid to asteroid within a system, does not need the original flora, fauna, or ore bodies. It needs reactors, fabricators, and the energy to run them. It converts what it finds into the tools required for the next conversion. Exploration ceases to be a search for pre-existing abundance and becomes the routine acquisition of mass and energy wherever they are cheapest to reach.

Under these conditions the classic advantage of rulers diminishes. Their historical leverage has been control over scarce, location-specific natural resources—the fertile valley, the mine, the fishery, the oil field. Those who hold the resource can extract tribute from those who need it. Once matter can be converted at acceptable cost, and once the energy to do so can be obtained from ubiquitous sources such as stellar output or fusion fuel, the specific deposit loses its unique value. A distant authority that once taxed the mine now finds that the mine can be replicated, or bypassed, by anyone with sufficient energy and the knowledge to apply it. What stays scarce is not the atoms but the organized capacity to rearrange them: engineering knowledge, capital equipment, and the institutions that keep both from being destroyed or seized by force.

Distance reinforces the shift. As [the earlier essay on distance](/future/2026/10/17/anarchy-across-the-stars.html) argued, projecting rule across light-years is expensive relative to the returns. When the returns themselves shrink because local conversion replaces dependence on any particular natural endowment, the calculation becomes still less favorable. What persists is the capacity of local groups to generate and apply energy, to convert the matter around them, and to trade the products with others who can do the same. Rulers retain an edge only where natural concentrations remain the cheapest source of energy or materials, or where they can still prevent others from developing the conversion technologies. Everywhere else, the binding constraint shifts from owning the resource to mastering the processes that make it unnecessary.
